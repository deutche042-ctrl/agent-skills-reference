## What a Storyboard Is
A storyboard is a preproduction tool for anime, film, and television. It plans the visual presentation of a story through a sequence of sketches or descriptions. A storyboard converts a written script into a visual sequence and defines each shot's content, composition, duration, and transition.

## Storyboard Types

### 1. Text Storyboard
Describes each shot's visual content, shot size, camera movement, sound effects, and other elements in text, making it easy for teams and AI video-generation tools to understand.

### 2. Image Storyboard
Uses sketches or detailed illustrations to show each shot and is common in traditional anime production.

### 3. Hybrid Storyboard
Combines image sketches with detailed written explanations and is the most comprehensive storyboard format.

## Shot Language

### Basic Composition Principles
- **Rule of thirds**: Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid and place important elements at intersections.
- **Centered composition**: Center the subject to emphasize it.
- **Golden ratio**: Arrange visual elements according to the golden ratio.
- **Leading lines**: Use lines to guide the viewer's eye.

### Visual Balance
- **Symmetrical composition**: Horizontal or vertical symmetry creates solemnity and stability.
- **Asymmetrical composition**: Creates dynamism and suits conflict.
- **Negative space**: Leave appropriate empty space to prevent visual crowding.

## Shot Sizes

### Long Shot
- **Purpose**: Show the environment and establish the setting.
- **Use**: Opening shots, transitions, and spatial relationships.
- **Example**: A wide view of the marquis's residence or garden.

### Full Shot
- **Purpose**: Show the character's full-body posture in relation to the surroundings.
- **Use**: Introducing a setting or character.
- **Example**: Shen Wanyu standing in the covered corridor.

### Medium Shot
- **Purpose**: Show the character's upper-body actions and expressions.
- **Use**: Dialogue and ordinary narrative scenes.
- **Example**: Lu Yanli and Shen Wanyu speaking.

### Medium Close-up
- **Purpose**: Show facial expressions and details.
- **Use**: Emotional expression and important dialogue.
- **Example**: Shen Wanyu looking troubled.

### Close-up
- **Purpose**: Emphasize a detail or intense emotion.
- **Use**: Eyes, hand movements, or key props.
- **Example**: Shen Wanyu's widened eyes or Lu Yanli's anguished expression.

### Extreme Close-up
- **Purpose**: Show a tiny detail from extremely close range.
- **Use**: Prop details, pupils, or tears.
- **Example**: A tear at the corner of Shen Wanyu's eye.

## Camera-Movement Techniques

### Static Shot
- **Characteristic**: The camera does not move.
- **Use**: Stable scenes, dialogue, and observation.
- **Effect**: Calm and objective.

### Dolly In
- **Characteristic**: The camera moves toward the subject.
- **Use**: Intensify emotion, focus on details, or build tension.
- **Example**: Move in toward Shen Wanyu's shocked face.

### Dolly Out
- **Characteristic**: The camera moves away from the subject.
- **Use**: Open the scene, reveal the environment, or release tension.
- **Example**: Pull back from Shen Wanyu's face to a full view of the room.

### Pan
- **Characteristic**: The camera rotates horizontally.
- **Use**: Follow a moving character or scan a setting.
- **Effect**: A fluid change in viewpoint.

### Tilt
- **Characteristic**: The camera rotates vertically.
- **Use**: Show height differences or move from top to bottom or bottom to top.
- **Example**: Look down from the ceiling at Lu Yanli in pain.

### Tracking Shot
- **Characteristic**: The camera follows a moving subject.
- **Use**: Walking or running characters.
- **Effect**: Immersion and participation.

### Handheld Shot
- **Characteristic**: Simulates the shake of a handheld camera.
- **Use**: Chaotic scenes, subjective viewpoints, and tense atmospheres.
- **Example**: Shen Wanyu's viewpoint when startled.

### Orbit Shot
- **Characteristic**: The camera circles the subject.
- **Use**: Reveal a 360-degree environment or emphasize the center.
- **Effect**: Strong drama and visual impact.

## Audio Design

### Sound-Effect Categories
- **Ambient sound**: Natural and background sounds, such as wind, rain, or birds.
- **Action sound**: Sounds generated by actions, such as footsteps, doors opening, or impacts.
- **Special effects**: System tones, magical effects, explosions, and similar sounds.
- **Foley**: Sounds added in postproduction, such as fabric movement or breathing.

### Music Types
- **Background music**: Music that continues through a scene.
- **Theme music**: Music associated with a character or theme.
- **Transition music**: Music used when changing scenes.
- **Mood music**: Music aligned with changes in emotion.

### Principles for Sound Effects
- **Clear layers**: Separate primary and background sounds clearly.
- **Rhythmic alignment**: Synchronize sound effects with visual rhythm.
- **Emotional reinforcement**: Use sound to strengthen the emotion of the image.
- **Avoid interference**: Do not compete with dialogue.

### Typical Sound-Effect Examples
- Burning candle: crackling
- Turning paper: rustling
- Opening a door: creaking
- Breathing: heavy panting
- System notification: a sustained electronic beep

## Emotion and Atmosphere

### Common Emotions
- **Joy**: Bright tones, upbeat music, and fast camera movement.
- **Sadness**: Cool tones, slow pacing, and soft music.
- **Anger**: Warm or red tones, rapid cuts, and aggressive sound effects.
- **Fear**: Dark tones, handheld shots, and sudden sound effects.
- **Oppression**: Low saturation, symmetrical composition, and heavy music.
- **Absurdity**: Strongly contrasting elements and discordant sound effects.

### Techniques for Creating Atmosphere
- **Lighting**: Light-dark contrast and color temperature.
- **Color**: Dominant palette and color symbolism.
- **Rhythm**: Cut speed and musical tempo.
- **Details**: Props, costumes, and environmental elements.

### Emotional Transitions
- **Gradual**: Emotion changes slowly.
- **Sudden**: Emotion shifts abruptly, such as celebration to horror.
- **Juxtaposed**: Two emotions coexist.
- **Contradictory**: Surface emotion conflicts with underlying emotion.

## Production Process

### Step 1: Analyze the Script
- Understand the story's theme and central conflict.
- Identify key scenes and turning points.
- Extract character information: appearance, personality, and behavior.
- Analyze setting requirements: environment, atmosphere, and time.

### Step 2: Plan the Shots
- Determine the shot size and duration of each shot.
- Design camera movements.
- Plan transitions.
- Preserve rhythm and continuity.

### Step 3: Design Visual Content
- Describe the principal elements in the frame.
- Define character positions and actions.
- Design composition and framing.
- Consider background and details.

### Step 4: Design Audio
- Design ambient and action sounds.
- Select the type of background music.
- Schedule sound-effect cues.
- Synchronize audio and visuals.

### Step 5: Set Emotion and Atmosphere
- Define the scene's emotional baseline.
- Select an appropriate palette and lighting.
- Design camera movement and editing rhythm.
- Strengthen emotional expression.

### Step 6: Format the Output
- Organize content according to the standard format.
- Check every required field.
- Ensure logical continuity.
- Validate the formatting.

## Adapting for AI Video Generation

### Control Storyboard Duration
- Keep each storyboard segment to 5–10 seconds.
- A Sora storyboard can use multiple time ranges, such as `[0–3 seconds]` and `[3–7 seconds]`.
- In the shot-prompt table, keep each shot to 1–3 seconds.

### Make Visual Descriptions Concrete
- Avoid abstractions; use specific, executable visuals.
- State character positions and actions explicitly.
- Specify camera angle and distance.
- Describe lighting and color.

### Simplify Text Descriptions
- Avoid complex long sentences.
- Use plain, direct language.
- Optimize for AI understanding and generation.
- Avoid ambiguity.

### Label Special Elements
- Mark special effects such as system text or flashes.
- Identify flashbacks with labels such as “flashback” or “filter.”
- Mark subjective viewpoints such as “heroine's POV.”
- State special camera techniques such as “montage.”

## Common Problems and Solutions

### Problem 1: Disjointed Shot Rhythm
**Solutions**:
- Check whether shot durations are appropriate.
- Ensure that changes in shot size are logical.
- Transition smoothly between camera movements.

### Problem 2: Unclear Emotion
**Solutions**:
- Communicate emotion through expressions and actions.
- Reinforce emotion with sound effects and music.
- Consider how lighting and color affect emotion.

### Problem 3: Insufficiently Specific Descriptions
**Solutions**:
- Add visual details.
- Define character positions and actions.
- Describe the environment and atmosphere.

### Problem 4: Nonstandard Formatting
**Solutions**:
- Follow the output-format template exactly.
- Check every required field.
- Ensure punctuation is correct.

## Summary
A storyboard is the critical step that converts a written script into visual images. A strong storyboard requires:
- Clear shot language
- Appropriate shot sizes and camera movements
- Precise audio design
- Accurate emotional expression
- Standardized output formatting

Following this guide produces professional, clear, comprehensible, and executable anime storyboards.
