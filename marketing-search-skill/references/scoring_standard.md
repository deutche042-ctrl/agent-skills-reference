# Reference Standard for Scoring Sources and Conclusions

This document provides a reference framework for evaluating source credibility and the strength of claims, helping ensure that analytical conclusions are rigorous and objective.

## Source Credibility Scoring Reference (0–5)

Evaluate each source across the following five dimensions:

| Dimension | Scoring Reference | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Authority** | 5: Official documents or regulatory announcements<br>4: Leading authoritative financial media<br>3: Established industry research firms or mainstream media<br>2: Influential industry experts or analysts<br>1: General media or independent creators<br>0: Anonymous sources or forum posts | Measures the credibility and background of the publisher. First-party information from official bodies and regulators receives the highest priority. |
| **Timeliness** | 5: Fully within the analysis window<br>3: Partly within the window or useful as background<br>1: Clearly outdated with little reference value | Assesses whether the information reflects current conditions. Timeliness is especially important for periodic data such as financial results. |
| **Cross-Verification** | 5: Confirmed by more than three independent authoritative sources<br>3: Confirmed by one or two independent sources<br>1: Isolated information with no corroboration<br>0: Clearly conflicts with other authoritative sources | Tests whether unrelated channels corroborate the information, which is essential for filtering out errors and bias. |
| **Originality** | 5: First-party information (such as financial filings, official press releases, or patent documents)<br>3: Secondary information that clearly cites and links to a primary source<br>2: Aggregated reporting or paraphrase without a primary source<br>1: Repeated retelling or unclear provenance | Determines whether information comes directly from the source event or has been interpreted and processed. First-party information is the most reliable. |
| **Expertise** | 5: Published by leading experts or institutions with deep industry knowledge<br>3: Published by practitioners or analysts with relevant expertise<br>1: Personal commentary from a non-expert<br>0: Content clearly lacks basic domain knowledge | Assesses the publisher's expertise in the relevant field. Specialist technical or financial analysis is stronger than broad commentary. |

## Overall Credibility and Conflict Handling

*   **Overall assessment**:
    *   **High credibility**: Typically scores highly on authority and originality and is cross-verified (for example, an SEC filing cited by several leading media outlets).
    *   **Medium credibility**: May be an opinion from an authoritative source or corroborated information that is not first-party (for example, an industry analyst's interpretation of a financial report).
    *   **Low credibility**: Usually isolated, poorly sourced, or lacking expertise (for example, an anonymous forum tip).

*   **Examples of synthesized claim strength**:
    *   **Strong claim**: An analytical conclusion supported by multiple high-credibility data sources, such as financial filings and official announcements.
    *   **Weak claim**: A hypothesis based on a single medium-credibility source, such as one analyst comment, or on rumors.

*   **Strategy for conflicting information**:
    1.  **Flag the disagreement**: When sources of comparable credibility contradict one another, clearly identify the conflict in the report and present each position and its supporting evidence.
    2.  **Trace the origin**: Prefer sources that are closer to the primary evidence and carry greater authority.
    3.  **Use less conclusive language**: For disputed points, use cautious wording such as “remains unclear” or “two possibilities exist” rather than making absolute claims.
