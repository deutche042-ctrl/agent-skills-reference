## Overview
SCQA—Situation, Complication, Question, Answer—is a classic McKinsey tool for framing problems and structuring narratives. A contextual opening quickly brings readers into the problem and makes the core hypothesis explicit.

## The Four SCQA Elements

### S — Situation
**Definition**: Describe background facts about the current state that everyone accepts.
**Purpose**: Establish common ground and prepare for the complication.
**Key points**:
- Factual: Use objective facts or widely recognized phenomena.
- Concise: Explain it in one or two sentences.
- Shared: Help readers relate quickly.

**Examples**:
- “Remote work has become mainstream over the past three years.”
- “Artificial intelligence is advancing at unprecedented speed.”
- “Short-form video has become a primary channel for distributing information.”

### C — Complication
**Definition**: Identify a pain point, change, or counterintuitive phenomenon within the current situation.
**Purpose**: Create tension, prompt reflection, and prepare the question.
**Key points**:
- Contrast: Create a contrast or contradiction with the situation.
- Urgency: Convey the importance and time sensitivity of the problem.
- Relevance: Connect to the reader's direct interests.

**Examples**:
- “However, 70% of employees report lower productivity after moving to remote work.”
- “Yet fewer than 30% of AI implementations succeed in practice.”
- “The fragmented nature of short-form video is weakening users' ability to read deeply.”

### Q — Question
**Definition**: The central question distilled from the situation and complication.
**Purpose**: Clarify the central issue the article will resolve.
**Key points**:
- Targeted: Address the complication directly.
- Answerable: Allow a clear answer within the scope of the article.
- Insightful: Make the question itself thought-provoking.

**Examples**:
- “How can remote-work productivity be improved?”
- “What are the critical success factors for implementing AI?”
- “How can people preserve deep-reading ability in the age of short-form video?”

### A — Answer (Core Hypothesis)
**Definition**: A preliminary core point based on professional knowledge and analysis.
**Purpose**: Set the direction for the article and guide the analysis that follows.
**Key points**:
- Insightful: Go beyond surface phenomena to reach the underlying issue.
- Verifiable: Support it with evidence later in the piece.
- Actionable: Make it convertible into specific recommendations.

**Examples**:
- “The primary cause of lower remote-work productivity is the lack of structured collaboration mechanisms, not physical distance itself.”
- “The key to implementing AI is contextual fit, not technical sophistication.”
- “In the age of short-form video, deep-reading ability must be rebuilt through active information filtering and time management.”

## Construction Process

### Step 1: Define the Situation (S)
**Method**:
- List three to five background facts related to the topic.
- Choose the fact that is best known and most likely to establish common ground.
- Describe it concisely in one or two sentences.

**Validation questions**:
- ✓ Is this an objective fact?
- ✓ Will readers readily accept it?
- ✓ Does it prepare for the complication?

### Step 2: Identify the Complication (C)
**Method**:
- Analyze pain points, contradictions, and changing trends within the situation.
- Identify counterintuitive phenomena or disruptive changes.
- Assess the urgency and importance of the complication.

**Validation questions**:
- ✓ Does it contrast with the situation?
- ✓ Is it compelling enough to attract attention?
- ✓ Is it closely related to the central issue?

### Step 3: Distill the Question (Q)
**Method**:
- Generate a list of possible questions from the situation and complication.
- Select the most representative and insightful question.
- Ensure it can be answered within the article's scope.

**Validation questions**:
- ✓ Does it directly address the complication?
- ✓ Does it point toward a clear answer?
- ✓ Is it worth thinking about?

### Step 4: Provide the Answer (A)
**Method**:
- Form an initial answer based on knowledge and experience.
- Ensure the answer is insightful and original.
- Check whether evidence can support it.

**Validation questions**:
- ✓ Does it go beyond surface phenomena?
- ✓ Can the later argument validate it?
- ✓ Does it have practical value?

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

### Mistake 1: An Overly Broad Situation
**Symptom**: The background is so broad and generic that it cannot create focus.
**Example**: “Humanity is entering the digital age.”
**Correction**: Focus on a specific field and phenomenon.
“In consumer markets, digital capabilities have become table stakes.”

### Mistake 2: A Complication without Tension
**Symptom**: The complication is ordinary and fails to draw attention.
**Example**: “Digital transformation faces some challenges.”
**Correction**: Use specific data and contrast.
“Yet 80% of corporate digital-transformation projects fail to deliver the expected returns.”

### Mistake 3: A Question That Misses the Point
**Symptom**: The question is not directly related to the complication.
**Example**: The complication is “lower productivity,” but the question asks, “How can employee satisfaction be improved?”
**Correction**: Make the question respond directly to the complication.
“How can the productivity drain in remote work be resolved?”

### Mistake 4: An Answer without Insight
**Symptom**: The answer remains superficial and lacks depth.
**Example**: “Improving efficiency requires stronger management.”
**Correction**: Offer a specific insight and direction.
“The core of the remote-work productivity drain is the structural absence of synchronous collaboration.”

## Classic Cases

### Case 1: Business Analysis
**Topic**: Analysis of new-retail trends
- **S**: Over the past decade, e-commerce has disrupted offline retail and steadily reduced store traffic.
- **C**: Yet recent data shows that experiential consumption in physical stores has grown 20% against the trend.
- **Q**: What is the core competitive advantage of physical retail in the e-commerce era?
- **A**: Physical retail's core value is shifting from “channel efficiency” to “experience value”; immersive experiences enabled by digital technology are becoming a new growth engine.

### Case 2: Product Analysis
**Topic**: Why are users willing to pay for memberships?
- **S**: Free content is the dominant business model on the internet.
- **C**: Yet paid subscriptions in video, music, and other categories have surpassed 500 million and continue to grow.
- **Q**: What fundamentally drives users to pay for memberships?
- **A**: Users are essentially buying a “predictable experience”—predictable quality, time, and service.

### Case 3: Workplace Analysis
**Topic**: The age-35 career crisis
- **S**: Age 35 is widely viewed as a workplace dividing line, after which promotion opportunities and job-switch success rates decline significantly.
- **C**: At the same time, however, the median age of corporate executives and core decision-makers has risen to 45.
- **Q**: What is the real career challenge at age 35?
- **A**: The essence of the age-35 crisis is not declining capability but a mismatch in value positioning—a delayed transition from executor to decision-maker.

## Applications
- **Business analysis articles**: Establish the entry point for analyzing industry trends.
- **Product thinking frameworks**: Define user pain points and product opportunities.
- **Strategic planning**: Clarify strategic questions and core hypotheses.
- **Academic writing**: Introduce the research question and contribution.
- **Consulting projects**: Align with clients on project scope and objectives.
